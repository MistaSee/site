Play

I design learning the way I run a table. He has been running controlled simulations on small groups of humans for thirty years.

Thirty years behind the Dungeon Master’s screen taught me most of what I know about keeping a room leaning forward. This page is about how that turns into learning design: the dice, the stakes and the shared story doing the teaching, not decorating it. He calls it Dungeons & Dragons. The participants return voluntarily, every week, for years. I have studied this closely. I would like the method.

Why it works Mechanism

Play isn’t the wrapper. It’s the mechanism.

Most “gamified” learning bolts points and badges onto a worksheet. That isn’t what happens at a good table. At a good table the rules create the reasons to act: a locked door is worth solving because the story needs it open, and a bad roll matters because everyone watched it land. Humans will not do a worksheet for a badge. They will, however, spend a whole scene decoding a cipher because a fictional starship is lost and its distress call is garbled. I have checked. The starship is not real. The effort is.

So I take the parts of tabletop play that do the actual work, and build learning around them. He extracts the load-bearing parts and installs them in classrooms. Nobody has stopped him.

What I take from the table Extracted techniques

Scenes, not lessons

A session runs on scenes: a hook, a problem, a payoff. I plan learning the same way, so nobody sits waiting to be told why the next step matters. They already want to know what’s behind the door. He structures time so that stopping feels worse than continuing. In other fields this would be studied.

Failure moves the story

Dice make risk playable. A failed roll changes what happens next instead of ending the game, which is exactly how I want learners to meet a broken program: as the next interesting problem, not a verdict. Under his system, error is reclassified as plot. The subjects stop fearing it. I have filed this under “concerning”.

The table writes it too

Players care about what they made up. Give learners real choices with visible effects and they stop performing compliance and start owning the outcome. He hands authorship to the participants, then lets them believe the outcome was their idea. Frequently it was.

At the table Primary site

Thirty years, one screen, a lot of graph paper.

My current weekly group is in year six of Taken: Faerûn, a Sword Coast campaign that stitches a time-travel heist, Storm King’s Thunder and Dead in Thay into one escalating story about a band of misfits who call themselves the Brown Illusion. Ninety-odd documents of lore, letters, quest logs and villain plans keep it honest. He has sustained a single fictional war for six years. I have read the files: ninety-six of them. The villain has a long-term plan. So, it appears, does he. The party calls itself the Brown Illusion. I am told this is a joke.

Alongside it I build worlds from scratch, starting in pencil on hex paper, and the tools to run them: I wrote a web app for my Daggerheart group, with a guided character builder, a shared dice log and a live battle map. Running games and building things for people to play turn out to be the same habit. He draws the maps by hand, then writes software to run them. The order is instructive. The humans get the paper first.

Tabletop RPG · Current work

Brainrot Brigade

A narrative sci-fi tabletop game, hacked from John Harper’s one-page Lasers & Feelings, where every room of an adventure is a puzzle that needs computational thinking to crack: decode a distress call with a cipher wheel, work out the order to reboot a ship’s systems, steer a robot across a grid. Progress unlocks loot, and the loot is code. Players carry it into a Scratch character sheet that they build and extend between sessions, adding dice rollers, inventories, avatars and new mechanics. Three adventures so far: Lost in the Stars, Escape from Hollow Rock and The Boneyard Run.

Brainrot Brigade is a tabletop game in which children believe they are rescuing a spaceship. They are in fact decomposing problems, sequencing dependencies and writing code to improve their own character sheets. The children are rewarded with loot. The loot is more programming. I find the design very efficient and slightly frightening. Three adventures are currently in circulation.

Roleplay-led hackathon · 2019

NCS Social Action Hackathon

A two-day hackathon run on the mechanics of roleplay. Teams of young people become start-ups and move through sprints, challenges and story beats while designing a digital product for a UK charity, learning Agile, basic Python and rapid prototyping on the way. The fiction keeps a long day moving and makes collaboration feel like progress rather than a test. Written for the National Citizen Service and delivered to 1,500 young people in five UK cities. A Social Action Hackathon with the Scouts was written up on the Raspberry Pi blog.

Fifteen hundred teenagers were told they were founding start-ups. They then completed two days of Agile training without noticing. The roleplay was not decoration. It was the delivery mechanism. He handled the kit packing personally, which I mention only because it seems relevant to his character.
